  <h1>Abiraterone: Uses, Dosage, Side Effects &amp; Patient Guide</h1>

  <p>Abiraterone is an oral androgen biosynthesis inhibitor used in the treatment of prostate cancer. Sold under the brand names <strong>Zytiga®</strong> and <strong>Yonsa®</strong>, it belongs to the CYP17 inhibitor class of medications and is prescribed alongside a low-dose corticosteroid to manage hormone-related side effects. My GLP Shot has put together this guide to help patients, caregivers, and clinicians understand how abiraterone works, how it is dosed, and what to watch for during treatment.</p>

  <p class="muted small">Last updated: <time datetime="2026-05-04">May 4, 2026</time>. This page is for informational purposes only. <strong>Always follow the guidance of your licensed oncologist or healthcare provider.</strong></p>

  <h2>What Is Abiraterone?</h2>
  <p>Abiraterone acetate is a small-molecule drug that selectively blocks an enzyme involved in androgen production. It is available as a tablet (Zytiga®) and as a micronized tablet formulation (Yonsa®) that can be taken with a low-fat meal. The FDA granted approval for use in metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC), and subsequent approvals extended its use to earlier disease settings in combination with androgen deprivation therapy (ADT).</p>
  <p>As a CYP17 inhibitor, abiraterone targets androgen synthesis at its source — not just in the testes, but also in the adrenal glands and within the tumor itself. This broader suppression is what distinguishes it from older hormonal approaches.</p>

  <h2>How Does Abiraterone Work?</h2>
  <p>Prostate cancer cells depend on androgens — primarily testosterone and dihydrotestosterone — to grow. Even after surgical or medical castration, the adrenal glands and the tumor microenvironment continue to produce androgens at levels sufficient to drive disease progression.</p>
  <p>Abiraterone inhibits the <strong>CYP17A1 enzyme</strong>, which catalyzes two critical steps in androgen biosynthesis: 17α-hydroxylase and 17,20-lyase activity. Blocking both steps sharply reduces androgen production across all three sources — the testes, adrenal glands, and intratumoral tissue.</p>
  <p>Because CYP17A1 inhibition also reduces cortisol production, the adrenal glands compensate by increasing ACTH secretion, which can raise mineralocorticoid levels and cause fluid retention, hypertension, and low potassium. This is why a concomitant corticosteroid (prednisone) is required during treatment — it suppresses ACTH and counteracts those mineralocorticoid effects.</p>

  <h2>Approved Indications &amp; Dosage Recommendations</h2>
  <p>Abiraterone is approved for use in adults with prostate cancer in the following settings:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC)</strong> — both in patients who have received prior docetaxel-based chemotherapy and in those who have not.</li>
    <li><strong>Metastatic high-risk castration-sensitive prostate cancer (mCSPC)</strong> — in combination with ADT.</li>
  </ul>

  <h3>Standard Dosage</h3>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Zytiga® (abiraterone acetate tablets):</strong> 1,000 mg orally once daily on an <em>empty stomach</em> — at least one hour before or two hours after eating.</li>
    <li><strong>Yonsa® (abiraterone acetate micronized tablets):</strong> 500 mg orally once daily with a low-fat meal.</li>
    <li><strong>Concomitant prednisone:</strong> 5 mg orally twice daily is required with both formulations to manage mineralocorticoid excess.</li>
  </ul>

  <h3>Hepatic Impairment Adjustments</h3>
  <p>Patients with moderate hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh B) require a dose reduction. Abiraterone is <strong>contraindicated</strong> in patients with severe hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh C) because the drug is extensively metabolized by the liver and exposure increases substantially with impaired hepatic function. Liver function should be assessed before starting treatment and monitored throughout.</p>

  <h2>Common Side Effects &amp; How to Manage Them</h2>
  <p>Most side effects of abiraterone stem from its mechanism — suppression of mineralocorticoids and androgens. The most frequently reported include:</p>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Hypertension</strong> — Monitor blood pressure at baseline and regularly throughout treatment. Antihypertensive therapy may be required.</li>
    <li><strong>Hypokalemia (low potassium)</strong> — Potassium levels should be checked regularly. Dietary potassium supplementation or potassium-sparing agents may be used under physician guidance.</li>
    <li><strong>Fluid retention and edema</strong> — Patients should report sudden weight gain or swelling. Diuretics may be considered if clinically appropriate.</li>
    <li><strong>Liver enzyme elevations (hepatotoxicity)</strong> — ALT and AST should be monitored every two weeks for the first three months, then monthly thereafter. Dose holds or discontinuation may be necessary if elevations are significant.</li>
    <li><strong>Fatigue</strong> — Common across all hormonal therapies for prostate cancer. Pacing activity and maintaining light exercise, where tolerated, can help.</li>
    <li><strong>Hot flashes and joint discomfort</strong> — Related to androgen suppression; generally manageable with supportive care.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>During the first month of treatment, blood pressure, serum potassium, and fluid status should be assessed at least every two weeks. After stabilization, monthly monitoring is typical, though your oncologist will set the schedule that fits your individual situation.</p>

  <h2>Drug Interactions &amp; Contra&#8209;indications</h2>
  <p>Abiraterone has a meaningful interaction profile that requires careful review of a patient's full medication list before and during treatment.</p>

  <h3>CYP Enzyme Interactions</h3>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Strong CYP3A4 inducers</strong> (e.g., rifampin, carbamazepine, phenytoin, St. John's Wort) — can significantly reduce abiraterone plasma concentrations. Avoid concurrent use where possible.</li>
    <li><strong>Strong CYP3A4 inhibitors</strong> (e.g., ketoconazole, itraconazole, clarithromycin) — may increase abiraterone exposure. Use with caution and monitor closely.</li>
    <li>Abiraterone itself inhibits <strong>CYP2D6</strong>, which means it can raise plasma levels of drugs metabolized by that enzyme — including certain antidepressants, antipsychotics, and opioids. Dose adjustments of those agents may be needed.</li>
  </ul>

  <h3>Other Notable Interactions</h3>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Anticoagulants</strong> — Monitor coagulation parameters more frequently if a patient is on warfarin or similar agents.</li>
    <li><strong>Antihypertensives</strong> — Abiraterone-induced hypertension may require adding or adjusting antihypertensive medications, which can interact with existing regimens.</li>
    <li><strong>Potassium-sparing diuretics</strong> — May be used to manage hypokalemia but require careful monitoring to avoid hyperkalemia.</li>
  </ul>

  <h3>Contraindications</h3>
  <ul>
    <li>Severe hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh C).</li>
    <li>Abiraterone is not approved for use in women and is contraindicated in pregnancy — it can cause fetal harm. Male patients with female partners of reproductive potential should use effective contraception during and for a period after treatment.</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>Patient Monitoring &amp; Follow&#8209;up Tests</h2>
  <p>Structured monitoring is essential during abiraterone therapy. The following framework reflects standard clinical practice, though your care team will tailor the schedule to your situation.</p>

  <h3>Baseline (Before Starting Treatment)</h3>
  <ul>
    <li>Complete metabolic panel (CMP) — including liver function tests (ALT, AST, bilirubin) and serum potassium</li>
    <li>Complete blood count (CBC)</li>
    <li>PSA and serum testosterone</li>
    <li>Blood pressure measurement</li>
    <li>Bone scan and CT imaging to establish disease extent</li>
  </ul>

  <h3>Ongoing Monitoring</h3>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Liver function tests (ALT, AST):</strong> Every two weeks for the first three months, then monthly. If elevations occur, increase frequency and consider dose modification.</li>
    <li><strong>Serum potassium and blood pressure:</strong> At least monthly; more frequently in the first month or if abnormalities are detected.</li>
    <li><strong>PSA:</strong> Typically every one to three months to assess treatment response.</li>
    <li><strong>Imaging:</strong> Bone scan and CT at intervals determined by your oncologist — usually every three to six months or when clinical progression is suspected.</li>
  </ul>

  <h3>When to Consider Dose Reduction or Discontinuation</h3>
  <ul>
    <li>ALT or AST rises above five times the upper limit of normal — hold treatment and recheck; discontinue if elevations persist or worsen.</li>
    <li>Uncontrolled hypertension or hypokalemia despite medical management.</li>
    <li>Radiographic or clinical disease progression — discuss next-line options with your oncologist.</li>
  </ul>
